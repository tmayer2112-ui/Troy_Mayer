EMG-Controlled Active Orthosis

Summary

A rehabilitative arm orthosis driven by the wearer's own muscle signals: flex to raise the forearm, extend to lower it, contract both to stop. I led the 5-engineer electronics team and owned the EMG board, the control firmware and the decoder.

Status

The team's first board (v1) was fabricated, and my v2 redesign was taken through a complete schematic. Every firmware and decoder number below comes from simulation or emulation driven by real recorded EMG, not from the board.

Control firmware

  • A C++17 core with no hardware dependencies, run by a 1 kHz FreeRTOS task on an ESP32-S3. The same file compiles into a simulator of the motor, transmission and forearm.
  • PI velocity loop on the motor encoder, IMC-tuned from a first-order motor model, with plant-inversion feed-forward and anti-windup, holding speed error to 0.8% while lifting the forearm.
  • A Bayesian EMG amplitude estimator with a 20 ms onset confirm, plus a faster control tier that unlocks once the firmware has measured the motor. On 24 real forearm contractions run through the controller and a simulated arm, the joint moves 43 ms after the muscle does (was 103 ms) and stops 32 ms after it relaxes (was 89 ms). The onset confirm takes false starts from motion artefacts to zero.
  • Ten runtime fault monitors (runaway, stall, electrode lead-off, tracking error, over-travel and more) latch the motor off. A hardware watchdog and a kill switch act independently of the code.
  • 26 GoogleTest unit, closed-loop and real-EMG latency tests, plus an ESP32-S3 build, run in CI.

v2 EMG Board

Four INA333 instrumentation-amp channels with a shared right-leg drive and a 23–474 Hz band-pass, centred on a buffered 1.65 V reference for the ESP32-S3's ADC. An ISO7763 digital isolator separates everything touching the body from the motor driver, and a hardware kill switch cuts the driver's enable line.

Decoder

I ran 115 days of real 4-channel forearm EMG through a sample-level emulation of each board. Cross-day 11-class accuracy averages 31% through v1; through v2 it holds at 87.3%, within a point of the 87.9% with no board at all. When a PyTorch CNN lost accuracy on later days, I traced it to electrode-polarity changes between sessions using per-channel skewness, and polarity augmentation brought it to 86.3%, matching the LDA baseline.

Tools: C++17, FreeRTOS, ESP32-S3, GoogleTest, KiCad, PyTorch, scikit-learn

Asteroid Hopping Robot

The problem

On a small asteroid, a robot gets around by hopping, and every hop is a gamble: push off too hard and the robot can escape the asteroid entirely; land badly and it tips over. The robot has to choose how far to go with both risks in mind.

What I'm building

A three-legged hopping robot that reshapes its legs between hops, and the control that carries it through each one:

  • Push-off: control that launches each hop, optimized for a target launch speed.
  • Flight: reaction-wheel control that reorients the body mid-flight.
  • Landing: knee impedance control that absorbs the impact.

Sizing each hop

In MuJoCo, I size each hop by comparing risk-limited policies that trade distance against the chance of escaping the asteroid or tipping over.

Test rig

I'm developing a pendulum rig to test landings.

Status

In progress since October 2026. Design and simulation are underway, so there are no results to report yet.

Tools: MuJoCo, impedance control, reaction-wheel attitude control, optimization

Personal project · Aug 2026 – Oct 2026

State Estimation and Control for a Legged Robot

The problem

A legged robot has no direct sensor for where its body is. IMU integration drifts within seconds, and there is no GPS indoors. But every time a foot is planted, that contact point is momentarily fixed in the world, which is a measurement if you model it correctly.

What I built

A contact-aided error-state extended Kalman filter in C++/Eigen (with a Python reference) for a simulated Unitree Go1. It propagates on the IMU, adds each planted foot to the state at touchdown, corrects with forward kinematics while the foot is down, and marginalizes it at liftoff.

  1. IMU at 1 kHz: angular velocity and linear acceleration.
  2. Error-state propagation: the nominal state is integrated and the error covariance propagated on the tangent space.
  3. Contact detection: foot force AND the gait schedule; the foot's position joins the state at touchdown.
  4. Leg-kinematics update: forward kinematics gives the base-to-foot vector; the foot is marginalized at liftoff.
  5. Error injection and reset: the correction is folded into the nominal state and the error reset to zero.

Results

  • Velocity RMSE 0.013 m/s against MuJoCo ground truth over a 60-second walk (mean of 10 sensor-noise seeds); IMU-only dead reckoning on the same walk gets 1.38 m/s. Final position error is about 0.15 m after 13.1 m walked.
  • Runs at 1 kHz, one update per IMU sample; the C++ step takes about 20 µs of the 1 ms budget.
  • Jacobians verified against finite differences in GoogleTest; the C++ filter matches the Python reference to 1e-13 on a recorded walk.
  • IMU noise and bias terms taken from the BMI088 data sheet, not tuned; contact parameters tuned on a separate walk with a different path.

Observability

With only an IMU and leg kinematics, roll, pitch and velocity stay bounded while position and yaw drift without limit: gravity is the only absolute direction the sensors can see. Derived analytically, then confirmed numerically (exactly four unobservable directions in every window tested). The weakest observable direction is tilt against accelerometer bias, which only separates once the robot turns.

Failure cases

  • About 2 cm/s of foot slip doubles the velocity error; the per-sample χ² gate never catches a smooth slide.
  • Force-only contact detection loses yaw fast as latency grows; gating it on the gait schedule holds yaw error under 0.6° even at 60 ms of latency.
  • A tutorial-style Q = 0.01·I loses attitude entirely; the data-sheet Q doesn't.

Running control on the estimate

The Raibert trot controller can run on the filter's estimate instead of ground truth. Fed the estimate, the robot completes 5 × 60 s walks without a fall, with velocity tracking within 0.004 m/s of the ground-truth-fed run. The trot controller and the estimator-to-controller loop are also in C++, matching the Python versions to 1e-12 rad.

The one new failure is a lean of about 4° before the first turn: tilt is only weakly observable while walking straight, and the controller levels the estimated trunk. A 4 s turn at startup makes tilt observable and removes it.

I also added a QP force-balance controller for the stance legs, in the style of MIT Cheetah 3's balance controller: one rigid body, with friction-limited foot forces solved as non-negative least squares at 500 Hz. Fed the estimate after the startup turn, it also completes 5 × 60 s walks without a fall.

Tools: C++, Eigen, Python, MuJoCo, GoogleTest, CMake

Electromechanical Optimization of Ionic Dielectric Gels

Summary

Raising the electromechanical force output of ionic dielectric gels for soft robotic actuators, through targeted chemical formulation and a custom automated test setup.

Two ways to measure

Flat samples sit under a Keyence laser displacement head, which reads sub-millimeter motion directly into LabVIEW. A cantilever is a different problem: the tip sweeps through an arc and leaves the laser's line, so there is nothing for it to track.

For those I wrote a Python computer-vision tracker that follows tip displacement frame by frame against a micrometer scale in the shot, covering the geometry the laser could not.

Approach and results

  • Built LabVIEW and MATLAB data acquisition, processing and normalizing datasets of 50,000+ points.
  • Iterated gel formulations for a force output increase of over 50%.
  • Designed 15 custom Fusion 360 test fixtures to standardize testing.
  • Tracked cantilever deflection to 0.01 mm on a 29.45 mm sample.

Tools: Python, LabVIEW, MATLAB, Fusion 360, direct-ink-write printing
